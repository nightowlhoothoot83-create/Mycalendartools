Eight phase bands

  • 🌑 New Moon — illuminated side mostly faces away from Earth
  • 🌒 Waxing Crescent — visible illumination increasing
  • 🌓 First Quarter — roughly half illuminated
  • 🌔 Waxing Gibbous — more than half and increasing
  • 🌕 Full Moon — near maximum illumination
  • 🌖 Waning Gibbous — more than half and decreasing
  • 🌗 Last Quarter — roughly half illuminated
  • 🌘 Waning Crescent — thin illumination decreasing

What this calculator is doing

The page estimates where a selected date falls within the Moon's roughly 29.53-day synodic cycle, then maps that position to one of eight familiar phase bands. That makes it useful for a quick visual answer such as “waxing crescent” or “waning gibbous”. It is deliberately simpler than an astronomical ephemeris.

What “illumination” means here

The percentages shown are rounded labels associated with the eight phase bands, not a precision calculation of the exact illuminated fraction at a particular second. Real illumination changes continuously. A date near the boundary between two bands can therefore look slightly different from a specialist astronomy service that calculates the Moon's exact geometry.

Useful real-world checks

A photographer can compare a planned landscape shoot with the broad lunar phase, a camper can get a rough idea of whether nights will be darker or brighter, and a teacher can use the date selector to show how the cycle progresses over several weeks. For a whole month, the Moon Phase Calendar is easier to scan than checking dates one by one.

Phase does not tell you when the Moon is visible

The phase alone does not provide moonrise, moonset, altitude, cloud cover or whether local terrain blocks the horizon. A full moon may be below your horizon at a particular time, while a crescent can be difficult to see even on a clear evening. For an actual observation session, pair the broad phase with location-specific rise and set times and a weather forecast.

About the “next full” and “next new” estimates

Those dates are rough cycle estimates derived from the same simplified phase model. They are useful for orientation, not for scheduling an observation that depends on the exact astronomical instant. For telescope work, eclipses, tides, navigation or scientific use, confirm exact phase times with an authoritative astronomy source.
